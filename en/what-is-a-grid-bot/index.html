What is a grid bot, and when does it work

A grid bot buys and sells on its own, at prices set in advance, while the price moves up and down. It makes money when the market goes sideways and loses when it trends away. Here's how it works, what you decide, and a real example with 100 Bitcoin bots from the past year.

The idea, in one picture

That's the whole trick: it doesn't try to guess where the price is going. It spreads buy orders below the current price and sell orders above it, and keeps refilling them. The more the price zigzags inside the range, the more cycles it completes and the more it earns.

What you decide

Range

The lowest and highest price it trades between. If the price leaves the range, the bot sits still until it comes back.

Number of grids

How many levels fit inside the range. More grids: more frequent cycles, each one earning less, and more fees.

Direction

On spot the bot starts partly in coins. On futures you pick long (earns more if it rises), short (if it falls) or neutral (neither).

Leverage

Futures only. It multiplies what the cycles earn, and the losses too. If the price moves far enough against you, the exchange liquidates the bot and you lose what you put in.

Where it wins and where it loses

A grid bot's result has two parts: the cycle profit, which always adds up, and the value of the position the bot holds at any moment, which can take away much more.

Works well

When the price goes back and forth inside the range for days. Every swing is a cycle banked, and at the end the price isn't far from where it started.

Works badly

When the price trends one way and doesn't come back. If it falls below the range, you're left holding coins bought higher. If it rises above, the bot has already sold everything and you miss the rally.

That's why a grid bot isn't easy money. Cycles give a steady trickle; a strong trend against you can take it all at once. And in a strong rally, simply buying and holding usually beats the bot.

Common beginner mistakes

  • A range that's too narrow. Lots of cycles at first, but the price soon leaves it and the bot stops with a losing position.
  • Too many grids. If the gap between levels is tiny, fees eat a big share of every cycle.
  • High leverage. At 10x or more, an ordinary week's move can liquidate the bot.
  • Trusting the annual return the exchange shows. It usually comes from the last few days, or from bots that did well. It says nothing about a bot you launch today.
  • Going long right when everyone is already long (or short when everyone is selling). That's when the price is most likely to turn against you.

FAQ

Does a grid bot always win?

No. It wins in a sideways market and loses when the price moves far from the range and doesn't come back. In the example above, 42 of 100 bots ended at a loss.

Spot or futures?

On spot there's no liquidation and the risk is that of holding the coin. On futures you can choose direction and leverage, but a strong move can liquidate the bot. To start, use spot or futures without leverage.

How many grids should I use?

Enough for frequent cycles, but with a gap between levels clearly larger than the round-trip fees. The oracle suggests a number for each pair based on its volatility, and the simulator lets you compare several.

When should I stop a bot?

Once the price leaves the range and doesn't look like coming back, the bot stops cycling and all you have left is the position. Decide in advance at what price you'd close it (the stop) so you don't improvise.
